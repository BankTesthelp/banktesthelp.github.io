Banking and Finance Concepts Every Bank Test Candidate Must Know

Almost every bank recruitment test in Pakistan includes a banking awareness section, and it is the section where non-commerce graduates lose easy marks. The good news: the concepts tested are few, basic and completely learnable in plain language — examiners are checking whether you understand how a bank works, not whether you hold a finance degree. This page explains the core concepts bank tests return to again and again. Read it slowly, then test yourself with the banking quizzes linked at the end.

Interest: Simple and Compound

In conventional banking, interest is the price of money: the borrower pays it, the depositor earns it. Simple interest is charged only on the original amount (the principal): Interest = Principal × Rate × Time. If Rs 10,000 is placed at 10% per year for 2 years, simple interest is 10,000 × 0.10 × 2 = Rs 2,000. Compound interest is charged on the principal plus the interest already earned: after year one the amount is Rs 11,000, and year two's interest is 10% of 11,000 = Rs 1,100, giving a total of Rs 2,100 — Rs 100 more than simple interest. That small gap, growing over time, is "interest on interest", and it is why compounding appears in tests so often. Islamic Banking: The Core Contracts

Islamic banking prohibits riba (interest) and instead earns through trade, partnership and leasing. Five contracts cover most test questions. Murabaha: the bank buys an asset and sells it to the customer at a clearly disclosed profit, paid in instalments. Musharakah: a partnership in which parties contribute capital and share profit and loss; in diminishing musharakah, the customer gradually buys the bank's share — a common home-finance structure. Mudarabah: one side provides the money, the other provides work and management; profit is shared by a pre-agreed ratio. Ijarah: leasing — the bank owns the asset and rents its use. Sukuk: certificates representing ownership in an asset or project, the Islamic counterpart in spirit (not in structure) to bonds. These concepts matter most for Islamic banks — our Meezan PBO pattern page shows how they are tested there.

Functions of the State Bank of Pakistan

The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) is the central bank, and its functions are among the most frequently tested facts in any banking section:

  • Issuing currency — it has the sole right to issue banknotes in Pakistan.
  • Monetary policy — it sets the policy rate to control inflation and support economic stability.
  • Regulating banks — it licenses, supervises and inspects banks and financial institutions.
  • Banker to the government and to banks — it manages government accounts and acts as lender of last resort.
  • Managing foreign exchange and reserves — it oversees the exchange-rate framework and the country's external reserves.
  • Promoting financial inclusion and development — programmes for agriculture, SMEs, housing and digital finance.

Types of Bank Accounts

Current accounts are for frequent transactions — businesses use them for daily payments; traditionally they earn no profit/interest because the money is fully withdrawable on demand. Savings accounts are for keeping money while earning a return, with some limits or conditions compared to current accounts. Term deposits lock money for a fixed period — one month to several years — in exchange for a higher expected return, with penalties or reduced return on early withdrawal. In Islamic banks, savings and term products are structured on Mudarabah, so the return is an expected profit rate rather than guaranteed interest. Everyday channels tested in banking sections include cheques, debit and credit cards, ATMs, mobile and internet banking, and interbank transfers such as IBFT and the national instant-payment system Raast.

Key Terms Worth Memoring

  • Policy rate: the interest rate set by the SBP that anchors all other rates in the economy.
  • KIBOR: the Karachi Interbank Offered Rate — the benchmark rate at which banks lend to each other, used to price many loans.
  • Inflation: the general rise in prices that reduces purchasing power; the SBP targets it through monetary policy.
  • Fiscal vs monetary policy: fiscal policy is the government's taxation and spending; monetary policy is the central bank's control of money and interest rates.
  • Remittances: money sent home by overseas Pakistanis — a vital source of foreign exchange.
  • Balance of payments: the record of a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world.
