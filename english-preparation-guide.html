English Preparation Guide for Bank Tests

English is the section that quietly decides most bank tests in Pakistan. It is usually the largest block on the paper, it appears in every bank's screening test, and it is the section where candidates from Urdu-medium backgrounds lose the most marks — not because their English is weak, but because they prepare it randomly. English rewards system. This guide gives you that system: what to study, in what order, and how to practise every day.

Know What the Section Contains

Bank-test English is built from a small set of question types: synonyms, antonyms, sentence correction and error spotting, fill-in-the-blanks (mostly prepositions and vocabulary), idioms, and one or two reading comprehension passages. That is good news — the syllabus is finite. You are not learning "English" in general; you are learning to answer six predictable question types quickly and accurately. Every hour of preparation should map to one of them. If a study activity does not clearly serve one of these six types, it is probably not the best use of your time this month.

Vocabulary Strategy: Fewer Words, Learned Properly

The biggest vocabulary mistake is downloading a list of 3,000 words and trying to memorize meanings in isolation. It does not stick, and it wastes weeks. Work instead with fifteen words a day, and learn each word four ways: its meaning, one synonym, one antonym, and one sentence of your own. A word you can use in a sentence is a word you will recognize in any question format.

Prioritize words that recur in formal and banking contexts — words like substantial, decline, facilitate, ensure, comply, estimate, vulnerable and transparent — and learn them in families: when you learn increase, also learn rise, surge, escalate and their opposites. Keep a small notebook or a notes app, and revise the whole list every Sunday. Spaced revision, not first-time learning, is what moves words into long-term memory. Test yourself regularly with the vocabulary-heavy English sets on our topics page.

Grammar Areas That Carry the Marks

Tenses

Most error-spotting questions are tense questions in disguise. Master the twelve tenses through their signal words: since and for point to perfect continuous forms, yesterday and ago to simple past, by the time to future or past perfect. Learn to spot the time marker first, then choose the tense — this single habit resolves a large share of sentence-correction items.

Subject–Verb Agreement

Learn the traps: collective nouns, phrases beginning with along with or as well as (the verb follows the first subject), each, every, either and neither (singular), and uncountable nouns like information, advice and furniture (always singular, never with an s). These few rules cover most agreement questions asked in bank tests.

Prepositions

Prepositions cannot be reasoned out — they must be absorbed as fixed pairs: interested in, responsible for, good at, afraid of, depend on, consist of, suffer from, married to. Build a personal list of fifty such pairs from the questions you practise, and read it aloud daily. In fill-in-the-blank questions, choose the preposition that forms a natural pair with the word beside it rather than translating from Urdu, which is where most errors begin.

Articles

Three rules handle most article questions: use a/an for a first, general mention (with an before vowel sounds — an hour, an honest officer); use the for specific, already-known or unique things (the State Bank, the sun); and use no article for general plural ideas (Banks play a vital role — not the banks when speaking generally). Practise spotting which of the three situations a sentence presents.

Reading Comprehension: Read the Questions First

Comprehension passages frighten candidates because they look long, but they are the most mechanical part of the paper. Use this approach: read the questions first, then skim the passage once with those questions in mind, then read properly and answer. Most questions are answered by locating a specific line, not by understanding the whole text deeply. Never answer from your own general knowledge — the passage is the only authority, and options often include true-in-real-life statements that the passage does not say. Reserve comprehension for a fixed time budget; it should never eat the minutes your grammar questions needed.

Synonyms and Antonyms: The Elimination Method

When you do not know a word, do not guess blindly. First, decide whether the word feels positive or negative in tone — this often eliminates two options immediately. Second, look at prefixes and roots: bene- suggests good (benefit, benevolent), mal- suggests bad (malfunction, malicious), -phobia means fear, -cide means killing. Third, for antonyms, beware the trap option that is actually a synonym — test setters place it there deliberately. This method converts hopeless guesses into fifty-fifty or better chances.

A Simple Daily Routine

  • 15 minutes: learn fifteen new words the four-way method described above.
  • 20 minutes: one grammar topic — revise the rule, then solve ten questions on it.
  • 15 minutes: one comprehension passage under a timer.
  • 10 minutes: revise yesterday's words and your preposition pairs.
  • Weekly: one full English quiz and a review of every mistake, logged by type.

An hour a day, done consistently for a month, transforms this section. Begin today: take an English practice quiz to set your baseline, follow the routine, and re-test each week.
