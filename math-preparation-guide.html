Quantitative & Math Preparation Guide for Bank Tests

The math section of a bank test is not advanced mathematics — it is school-level arithmetic performed under pressure, without a calculator, at roughly one minute per question. That makes it the most trainable section on the paper: the question types are few, the formulas are short, and speed comes from method rather than talent. This guide covers the six areas that produce most bank-test math questions, each with a fully worked example created for this guide. Learn the method in each example, then drill it on the topic quizzes until it becomes automatic.

1. Percentages: The Foundation of Everything Else

Nearly every other topic — profit and loss, interest, discounts, data interpretation — is percentages in disguise. Two skills matter: finding a percentage of a quantity, and finding the percentage change between two values. Memorize the common fraction equivalents (50% = 1/2, 25% = 1/4, 20% = 1/5, 12.5% = 1/8, 10% = 1/10) and most calculations become mental.

Worked example: A branch's deposits rise from Rs 45,000 thousand to Rs 51,750 thousand in a year. What is the percentage increase?
Increase = 51,750 − 45,000 = 6,750. Percentage increase = 6,750 ÷ 45,000 × 100 = 15%. Always divide by the original value, not the new one — that is the trap in the options.

Worked example: A cashier counts Rs 240,000, of which 12.5% is in Rs 100 notes. How many Rs 100 notes are there?
12.5% = 1/8, so 1/8 × 240,000 = Rs 30,000. Number of notes = 30,000 ÷ 100 = 300 notes.

2. Ratios and Proportion

Ratio questions almost always reduce to one move: add the parts, divide the total by that sum to find one part, then multiply back. Keep the order of the ratio matched to the order of the names in the question.

Worked example: A bonus of Rs 84,000 is divided between two officers in the ratio 5 : 7. How much does each receive?
Total parts = 5 + 7 = 12. One part = 84,000 ÷ 12 = Rs 7,000. First officer: 5 × 7,000 = Rs 35,000. Second officer: 7 × 7,000 = Rs 49,000. Check: 35,000 + 49,000 = 84,000 ✓.

3. Averages

Average = total ÷ count. The test's favourite twist is the reverse question: given the average and all values but one, find the missing value by rebuilding the total.

Worked example: A branch records deposits (in thousands) of 120, 150, 90, 180 and 160 on five working days. What is the daily average?
Total = 120 + 150 + 90 + 180 + 160 = 700. Average = 700 ÷ 5 = 140 thousand.
Twist: if the six-day average must be 150, the sixth day needs a total of 6 × 150 = 900, so day six must bring 900 − 700 = 200 thousand.

4. Profit and Loss

Profit and loss percentages are always calculated on the cost price, never on the selling price. Learn the two formulas: Profit % = (Profit ÷ Cost Price) × 100, and Selling Price = Cost Price × (1 + Profit%/100) for a profit, or × (1 − Loss%/100) for a loss.

Worked example: A shopkeeper buys an item for Rs 800 and sells it for Rs 920. Find the profit percentage.
Profit = 920 − 800 = Rs 120. Profit % = 120 ÷ 800 × 100 = 15%.

Worked example: An item costing Rs 2,500 is sold at a loss of 15%. What is the selling price?
Selling price = 2,500 × (1 − 0.15) = 2,500 × 0.85 = Rs 2,125.

5. Interest: Simple and Compound

Simple interest is one formula: SI = (Principal × Rate × Time) ÷ 100. Compound interest is the same idea applied year by year — each year, interest is calculated on the grown amount. For two years, simply multiply by (1 + rate) twice.

Worked example (simple): Find the simple interest on Rs 200,000 at 9% per annum for 3 years.
SI = (200,000 × 9 × 3) ÷ 100 = Rs 54,000.

Worked example (compound): Find the compound interest on Rs 100,000 at 10% per annum for 2 years.
After year 1: 100,000 × 1.10 = Rs 110,000. After year 2: 110,000 × 1.10 = Rs 121,000. Interest = 121,000 − 100,000 = Rs 21,000. Notice it exceeds the simple interest (Rs 20,000) by Rs 1,000 — that difference is the interest earned on the first year's interest, a favourite test question in itself.

6. Time and Work

The whole topic rests on one idea: if a person finishes a job in n days, their daily work is 1/n of the job. Add the daily rates to combine workers, then invert to get the days.

Worked example: Officer A can process a file batch in 12 days and Officer B in 15 days. Working together, how long will they take?
A's daily work = 1/12, B's = 1/15. Together: 1/12 + 1/15 = 5/60 + 4/60 = 9/60 = 3/20 of the job per day. Time = 1 ÷ (3/20) = 20/3 ≈ 6.7 days. Shortcut for two workers: time = (12 × 15) ÷ (12 + 15) = 180 ÷ 27 = 6.7 days — same answer, faster.

How to Practise for Speed

Knowledge is only half of this section; the other half is pace. Practise in timed blocks of fifteen questions and aim to finish with a minute to spare. Do rough work neatly in columns so you can re-check quickly. When a calculation turns ugly, estimate and eliminate options — bank-test options are usually spaced widely enough for estimation to work. And never let one question consume three minutes: mark it, move on, and return if time remains.

Work through these six areas in order, one per day, redoing each worked example with the numbers changed until the method feels boring. Then take a timed math practice quiz, log every error by topic, and drill the weakest area again. Within a few weeks, the section that scared you becomes the section you finish first — and when you are ready to mix it with the other sections under real conditions, move on to a full-length test.
